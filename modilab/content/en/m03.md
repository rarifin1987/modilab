### Learning objectives
After completing this module you should be able to:
1. explain the purpose of periodic boundary conditions and the minimum image convention,
2. use reduced units and convert them to real units,
3. assess the limitations of system size in a simulation.

### Concepts
One cubic centimetre of liquid argon contains roughly $10^{22}$ atoms, while an educational simulation handles only a few hundred. If the atoms were placed in a box with walls, most of them would be near a wall and the properties would be dominated by surface effects. The solution is **periodic boundary conditions**: the simulation box is treated as if it were repeated endlessly in every direction, like floor tiles. An atom leaving one side immediately re-enters from the opposite side, and every atom interacts with copies of its neighbours.

So that each pair is counted only once, the **minimum image convention** is used: the distance between atoms $i$ and $j$ is taken from the copy of $j$ that is closest. In the MoDiLab code a single line does this:

$$\Delta\mathbf r \leftarrow \Delta\mathbf r - L\,\mathrm{round}(\Delta\mathbf r / L)$$

As a consequence, the interaction cutoff $r_c$ must not exceed $L/2$.

**Reduced units.** The simulation computes in units of σ, ε and m, so all numbers are of order one and a single simulation applies to every Lennard-Jones atom. Conversion to real units is done at the end:

$$T = T^*\frac{\varepsilon}{k_B}, \qquad t = t^*\,\sigma\sqrt{\frac{m}{\varepsilon}}, \qquad \rho = \rho^*\frac{m}{\sigma^3}, \qquad P = P^*\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma^3}$$

Try switching the **display units** in the left panel to *Real* and changing the reference element. Every plot in this and the other modules will change its units, while the shape of the curves stays the same. Why is that?

### Analysis questions
1. Watch the red atom as it crosses the edge of the box. Is any atom actually lost or gained?
2. Why can one simulation in reduced units describe both neon and xenon? What distinguishes their results?
3. Using the calculator, find how many picoseconds a 3000 step simulation with $\Delta t^* = 0.005$ covers for argon. Is this time span enough to observe atomic vibrations in a solid, whose periods are of the order of picoseconds?
