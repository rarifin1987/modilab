### Learning objectives
After completing this module you should be able to:
1. define and interpret the radial distribution function $g(r)$,
2. calculate the coordination number from $g(r)$,
3. distinguish gases, liquids and solids by their atomic structure.

### Concepts
How can we "see" the structure of matter in thousands of atomic coordinates? The main tool is the **radial distribution function** $g(r)$: the ratio between the average density of atoms at distance $r$ from an atom and the average density of the whole system. For an ideal gas $g(r) = 1$ everywhere. A value $g(r) > 1$ means atoms are found more often at that distance.

Integrating $g(r)$ gives the **coordination number**, the number of neighbours within radius $r$:

$$n(r) = \rho\int_0^r g(r')\,4\pi r'^2\,dr' \quad (3D), \qquad n(r) = \rho\int_0^r g(r')\,2\pi r'\,dr' \quad (2D)$$

The value of $n(r)$ at the first minimum of $g(r)$ is the number of nearest neighbours. It is 6 for the 2D hexagonal lattice and 12 for the 3D fcc lattice. Many important metals such as Al, Cu, Ni, Ag and Au have the fcc structure, so this concept applies directly to engineering materials.

The signature of each phase in $g(r)$:
* **Solid**: sharp peaks repeating to long distances because atoms are arranged in order.
* **Liquid**: the first peak is still clear, but later peaks quickly decay towards 1. A liquid is ordered only locally.
* **Gas**: a single low peak that quickly approaches 1.

$g(r)$ is not only a simulation quantity. It is directly related to the structure factor measured in X ray and neutron diffraction experiments, so simulation results can be compared with laboratory data.

### Analysis questions
1. In the solid phase, compare the position of the first peak with the nearest neighbour distance of the perfect lattice. Why is the peak broadened even though the lattice is ordered?
2. Compare the first shell coordination numbers of the liquid and the solid, in both 2D and 3D. Do the results match your expectation? Why can a 3D liquid have almost as many nearest neighbours as the solid even though its arrangement is disordered? (Hint: also compare the width of the first g(r) peak and the distance to its first minimum.)
