### Learning objectives
After completing this module you should be able to:
1. calculate temperature from atomic velocities using the equipartition theorem,
2. distinguish the NVE and NVT ensembles,
3. explain how a thermostat works and the effect of its coupling time.

### Concepts
Temperature is not a property of a single atom but a measure of the average kinetic energy of many atoms. According to the **equipartition theorem**, each translational degree of freedom holds an average energy of $\tfrac12 k_BT$, so

$$k_B T = \frac{1}{N_f}\sum_{i=1}^{N} m v_i^2, \qquad N_f = dN - d$$

where $d$ is the number of dimensions. The subtraction of $d$ arises because the total momentum of the system is set to zero.

A simulation that only follows Newton's laws keeps the number of particles N, the volume V and the total energy E constant. This collection of states is called the **microcanonical ensemble (NVE)**. In NVE the temperature can change, for example when potential energy turns into kinetic energy as a crystal melts.

Real experiments are usually carried out at constant temperature because the sample is in contact with its surroundings. This is the **canonical ensemble (NVT)**, imitated with a **thermostat**. The Berendsen thermostat multiplies all velocities by the factor

$$\lambda = \sqrt{1 + \frac{\Delta t}{\tau_T}\left(\frac{T_0}{T} - 1\right)}$$

so that the temperature approaches the target $T_0$ exponentially with time constant $\tau_T$. The Berendsen thermostat is easy to understand but does not produce an exactly canonical distribution. Research simulations usually use Nosé-Hoover or Langevin thermostats.

### Analysis questions
1. In NVE, why is the final temperature different from the initial temperature even though the total energy is constant?
2. Change $\tau_T$ from 0.05 to 1.0. How does the time needed to reach the target temperature change? What is the risk if $\tau_T$ is too small?
3. Reduce the number of particles. How do the temperature fluctuations change? Compare with the theoretical expectation that relative fluctuations scale as $1/\sqrt{N}$.
